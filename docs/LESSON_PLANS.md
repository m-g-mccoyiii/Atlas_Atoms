# Atlas' Atoms: 20 Lesson Plans

A path from "what is an atom?" to burning fuel, acids and the chemistry of your body. Each lesson takes 15 to 30 minutes and is built around the game. The same lessons appear in the game under **Lessons**, and the game ticks a lesson off automatically when the child finishes its activity.

**For the adult:** let the child do the building. Ask "what do you think will happen?" before each step. The off-screen activities use ordinary household items. Do them with an adult nearby, and never taste or sniff unknown chemicals.

## Lesson 1: Everything is made of atoms

*Start here · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** Everything you can touch, from your desk to your dog, is built from tiny pieces called atoms. Atoms are far too small to see. A single human hair is hundreds of thousands of atoms wide.

**Words**

- **atom**: The tiny building block of everything.
- **matter**: Anything that takes up space and has weight.

**Try it in the game**

1. Open the Atoms tray on the left and drag one hydrogen atom onto the bench.
2. Click the Hydrogen button (don't drag) to open its element card.
3. Turn the view with your mouse. Zoom with the scroll wheel.

**Check yourself**

- Can you see atoms with your eyes?  
  *Answer: No. They are far too small. Scientists use special tools, and games like this one, to picture them.*
- Name three things made of atoms.  
  *Answer: Anything works: air, water, a chair, you.*

**In real life.** The air in the room, the water you drink and your own body are all atoms arranged in different ways.

**Away from the screen.** Look around the room and list 10 things. Each one is made of atoms.

## Lesson 2: Elements and their symbols

*Start here · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** An element is one kind of atom. Each element has a short symbol: H is hydrogen, C is carbon, N is nitrogen and O is oxygen. There are about 118 known elements.

**Words**

- **element**: A pure kind of atom, like all-hydrogen or all-oxygen.
- **symbol**: A one- or two-letter code for an element.

**Try it in the game**

1. Click each of H, C, N and O in the tray to read their cards.
2. Look at the colors: the game uses the colors chemists use for these atoms.
3. Find the atomic number on each card. It is the number of protons in the nucleus.

**Check yourself**

- What is the symbol for oxygen?  
  *Answer: O.*
- What is the symbol for carbon?  
  *Answer: C.*

**In real life.** The symbols are the same in every language. A chemist in any country knows what H₂O means.

**Away from the screen.** Make flash cards for H, C, N and O with the symbol on one side and the name on the other.

## Lesson 3: Atoms have hands

*Start here · about 20 minutes*

**Big idea.** Each kind of atom wants a certain number of bonds. In the game these are its hands: hydrogen has 1, oxygen has 2, nitrogen has 3 and carbon has 4. Chemists call this number the valence.

**Words**

- **bond**: A link between two atoms.
- **valence**: How many bonds an atom likes to make.

**Try it in the game**

1. Drag two hydrogen atoms onto the bench.
2. Drop one on top of the other to make a bond.
3. Watch the dots under each atom. Green dots are used hands. When every hand is used the atom smiles.
4. Open the Atlas to see that you found Hydrogen gas.

**Check yourself**

- How many hands does oxygen have?  
  *Answer: Two.*
- Why did the two hydrogens smile?  
  *Answer: Each hydrogen has one hand, and the bond used it.*

**In real life.** Hydrogen gas (H₂) is what fills some balloons and fuels some rockets.

**Away from the screen.** Hold hands with a friend. You are each using one 'hand' to bond, like two hydrogens.

## Lesson 4: Sharing makes a bond

*Start here · about 20 minutes*

**Big idea.** A bond forms when two atoms share electrons. Sharing one pair makes a single bond. Sharing two pairs makes a double bond. Oxygen in the air is two oxygen atoms joined by a double bond.

**Words**

- **electron**: A tiny particle that moves around the nucleus. Atoms share them to bond.
- **double bond**: Two shared pairs of electrons between the same two atoms.

**Try it in the game**

1. Drop one oxygen onto another oxygen to make one bond.
2. Drop one on the other again. The bond becomes a double bond and both atoms smile.
3. Switch to the Electrons view and find the two lines between the O atoms.

**Check yourself**

- What do atoms share in a bond?  
  *Answer: Electrons.*
- How many lines are between the atoms in O₂ in the Electrons view?  
  *Answer: Two, for a double bond.*

**In real life.** The oxygen you breathe is O₂, with exactly this double bond.

**Away from the screen.** Take two paper clips and link them once, then twice with a rubber band. Which is harder to pull apart?

## Lesson 5: Your first molecule: water

*Start here · about 20 minutes*

**Big idea.** Atoms bonded together make a molecule. A formula tells you how many of each atom. H₂O means 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom. The small number after a letter says how many.

**Words**

- **molecule**: Two or more atoms bonded together.
- **formula**: A short way to write which atoms are in a molecule and how many.

**Try it in the game**

1. Drag one oxygen onto the bench.
2. Drop two hydrogens on it, one at a time.
3. Look at the formula in the 'On your bench' panel. It should say H₂O.
4. Poke an atom by clicking it. Read what the game tells you.

**Check yourself**

- What does the 2 in H₂O mean?  
  *Answer: There are 2 hydrogen atoms.*
- How many atoms are in one water molecule?  
  *Answer: Three: two hydrogens and one oxygen.*

**In real life.** Oceans, rain and about 60% of an adult human body are water.

**Away from the screen.** Fill a glass of water. Everything you see is billions upon billions of H₂O molecules.

## Lesson 6: Shape matters

*Building · about 25 minutes*

**Big idea.** Molecules have shapes, and shape comes from how the bonds and lone pairs push on each other. Water is bent. Carbon dioxide is a straight line.

**Words**

- **lone pair**: A pair of electrons on an atom that is not used in a bond.
- **linear**: Shaped like a straight line.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build water. Rotate it and see the bend.
2. Switch to Orbitals. The gold lobes on oxygen are lone pairs. They push the two hydrogens closer together.
3. Build CO₂: one carbon with a double bond to each of two oxygens. Look at its shape from every side.

**Check yourself**

- Is water bent or straight?  
  *Answer: Bent, about 104.5°.*
- Why is CO₂ straight?  
  *Answer: Carbon has no lone pairs, and the two double bonds spread as far apart as they can.*

**In real life.** Water's bent shape is part of why ice floats and why salt dissolves in water.

**Away from the screen.** Use two marshmallows and a toothpick or two to make a straight line and a bent shape.

## Lesson 7: The air around you

*Building · about 25 minutes*

**Big idea.** Air is mostly two molecules: nitrogen (N₂, about 78%) and oxygen (O₂, about 21%). A little is argon, and a tiny amount is carbon dioxide (CO₂, about 0.04%).

**Words**

- **gas**: A state of matter where molecules fly around freely.
- **triple bond**: Three shared pairs of electrons between the same two atoms.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build N₂: drop one nitrogen on another three times to make a triple bond.
2. Compare the nitrogen and oxygen bonds in the Electrons view.
3. Open your Atlas and look at the real-life notes for N₂, O₂ and CO₂.

**Check yourself**

- What is most of the air made of?  
  *Answer: Nitrogen, N₂.*
- Why does nitrogen rarely react?  
  *Answer: Its triple bond is very strong.*

**In real life.** You breathe in O₂ and breathe out CO₂, and each breath has mostly nitrogen in it.

**Away from the screen.** Wave a piece of paper. You are pushing through air, the molecules you just built.

## Lesson 8: Carbon, the builder

*Building · about 25 minutes*

**Big idea.** Carbon makes four bonds, so it can connect to other atoms in many directions. That makes it the backbone of almost all of the molecules in living things.

**Words**

- **tetrahedron**: A pyramid-like shape with four faces. Four bonds on carbon point to its corners.
- **organic chemistry**: The chemistry of carbon compounds.

**Try it in the game**

1. Drop four hydrogens on one carbon to build methane, CH₄.
2. Rotate it. Notice the hydrogens point to the corners of a tetrahedron.
3. Check the angle: all four bonds are about 109.5° apart.

**Check yourself**

- How many bonds does carbon make?  
  *Answer: Four.*
- What is the formula for methane?  
  *Answer: CH₄.*

**In real life.** Methane is the main part of natural gas used in many stoves and furnaces.

**Away from the screen.** Build a tetrahedron from 4 gumdrops and 6 toothpicks.

## Lesson 9: Chains of carbon

*Building · about 25 minutes*

**Big idea.** Carbon atoms can bond to each other to make chains. Add hydrogens to fill all the hands. Longer chains are heavier. That is one reason propane and butane are gases that can be squeezed into a liquid, and why gasoline, with longer chains, is a liquid.

**Words**

- **chain**: Atoms bonded one after another in a line.
- **fuel**: A material that is burned for energy.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build propane (three carbons in a row, with hydrogens on all the other hands).
2. Build butane with four carbons.
3. Look at the zig-zag. Why isn't it straight?

**Check yourself**

- How many hydrogens are in propane?  
  *Answer: Eight: C₃H₈.*
- Why does the chain zig-zag?  
  *Answer: Each carbon's four bonds point to the corners of a tetrahedron, so the angles are about 109.5°.*

**In real life.** Propane fuels camp stoves and grills. Butane is in many lighters.

**Away from the screen.** Link paper clips into a chain, then try to make a straight chain. It naturally bends.

## Lesson 10: Double and triple bonds

*Building · about 25 minutes*

**Big idea.** Carbon atoms can share more than one pair. Double and triple bonds are shorter and stronger. A C=C double bond is flat, and a C≡C triple bond is a straight line.

**Words**

- **unsaturated**: A molecule with a double or triple bond between carbons.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build ethylene, C₂H₄: two carbons joined by a double bond, with two hydrogens on each.
2. Build acetylene, C₂H₂, with a triple bond.
3. Rotate each. Which one is flat? Which one is a straight line?

**Check yourself**

- Which is shorter, C–C or C=C?  
  *Answer: C=C. More shared pairs pull the atoms closer.*
- How many hydrogens are in acetylene?  
  *Answer: Two.*

**In real life.** Ripening fruit gives off ethylene. Welding torches burn acetylene.

**Away from the screen.** Put a ripe banana in a paper bag with a green tomato. Check each day.

## Lesson 11: Same atoms, different molecule

*Exploring · about 30 minutes*

**Big idea.** The same atoms can connect in different ways to make different molecules. These are called isomers. Ethanol and dimethyl ether both have the formula C₂H₆O, but ethanol is a liquid at room temperature and dimethyl ether is a gas.

**Words**

- **isomer**: A molecule with the same atoms as another but a different arrangement.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build C₂H₆O as ethanol: C–C–O with the O at the end holding an H.
2. Now build it a different way: C–O–C. Look at the 'On your bench' panel. It says right atoms, different arrangement.
3. Try making two other arrangements of the same atoms.

**Check yourself**

- Can two different molecules have the same formula?  
  *Answer: Yes. Arrangement matters.*
- What is an isomer?  
  *Answer: A molecule with the same atoms as another but joined in a different way.*

**In real life.** Medicines often depend on exactly how atoms are arranged. A small change in arrangement can change how a molecule acts.

**Away from the screen.** Build two different shapes from the same 6 LEGO bricks.

## Lesson 12: Salt: a different kind of bond

*Exploring · about 25 minutes*

**Big idea.** Not all bonds are shared electrons. In salt, sodium gives an electron to chlorine. Sodium becomes positive, chlorine becomes negative, and opposite charges attract. This is an ionic bond. The game draws it as a dashed line.

**Words**

- **ion**: An atom with an electric charge because it gained or lost electrons.
- **ionic bond**: An attraction between positive and negative ions.
- **covalent bond**: A bond where atoms share electrons.

**Try it in the game**

1. Find enough molecules to unlock sodium and chlorine in the tray.
2. Drop sodium onto chlorine.
3. Look for the dashed line and the + and − signs. Compare with a water bond.

**Check yourself**

- What does sodium do with its electron?  
  *Answer: Gives it to chlorine.*
- What is different about an ionic bond and a covalent bond?  
  *Answer: Ionic bonds come from giving and taking an electron. Covalent bonds share electrons.*

**In real life.** Table salt is sodium chloride. In a real grain of salt, huge numbers of ions stack in a crystal.

**Away from the screen.** Stir a spoon of salt into a glass of warm water until it disappears. The salt ions spread out among the water molecules. Do not drink it.

## Lesson 13: Acids in the kitchen

*Exploring · about 25 minutes*

**Big idea.** Acids taste sour. Vinegar has acetic acid, lemons have citric acid, and fizzy water has a little carbonic acid. Many acids contain a –COOH group, which can give away its hydrogen.

**Words**

- **acid**: A substance that can give away a hydrogen ion.
- **functional group**: A small group of atoms, like –COOH, that gives a molecule its special behavior.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build acetic acid, CH₃COOH.
2. Find the C(=O)OH group in the Electrons view.
3. Build carbonic acid and compare.

**Check yourself**

- What is the sour part of vinegar?  
  *Answer: Acetic acid.*
- Name an acid you can find in a fruit.  
  *Answer: Citric acid in lemons and limes.*

**In real life.** Acids add tang to food and drinks. Never taste an unknown chemical.

**Away from the screen.** With an adult, put a spoon of baking soda in a cup and add a little vinegar. Watch the bubbles. They are carbon dioxide gas.

## Lesson 14: Heat makes atoms move

*Exploring · about 20 minutes*

**Big idea.** Temperature is a measure of how fast the particles in something are moving. In a solid they jiggle in place. In a liquid they slide past each other. In a gas they fly freely.

**Words**

- **temperature**: How fast particles are moving, on average.
- **solid, liquid, gas**: The three common states of matter.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build a water molecule and put a few more on the bench.
2. Slide the Heat slider all the way up and watch the atoms shake.
3. Press Shake. Then turn Heat down. How does the motion change?

**Check yourself**

- What happens to particles when something gets hotter?  
  *Answer: They move faster.*
- In which state do particles move the most freely?  
  *Answer: Gas.*

**In real life.** Ice melts because heat makes water molecules move enough to slide past each other.

**Away from the screen.** Put an ice cube in a warm bowl and a cold bowl. Which melts faster, and why?

## Lesson 15: Reactions rearrange atoms

*Exploring · about 25 minutes*

**Big idea.** In a chemical reaction, bonds break and new bonds form. The atoms are the same, but they end up in new molecules.

**Words**

- **reaction**: A change where atoms are rearranged into new molecules.
- **reactant**: A molecule you start with.
- **product**: A molecule you end up with.

**Try it in the game**

1. Discover hydrogen, oxygen and water.
2. In the Atlas, put two H₂ and one O₂ on the bench.
3. Press Spark in the Reactions panel and watch the atoms move into new places.

**Check yourself**

- What are the reactants in 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O?  
  *Answer: Hydrogen and oxygen.*
- What is the product?  
  *Answer: Water.*

**In real life.** Rocket engines can burn hydrogen with oxygen. The only product is water.

**Away from the screen.** Take apart a LEGO model and build a different one from the same bricks. That is a reaction.

## Lesson 16: Atoms never disappear

*Exploring · about 25 minutes*

**Big idea.** In every reaction, the number of each kind of atom stays the same. Atoms are rearranged, not made or destroyed. This is called conservation of mass.

**Words**

- **conservation of mass**: Matter is not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.
- **balanced equation**: An equation with the same atoms on both sides.

**Try it in the game**

1. Run two different reactions from the Reactions panel.
2. Before each Spark, count the atoms on the bench by element.
3. After Spark, count again. They should match.

**Check yourself**

- Do the atoms change in a reaction?  
  *Answer: No. Only how they are connected changes.*
- How many oxygen atoms are in 2 H₂O?  
  *Answer: Two.*

**In real life.** Chemists count atoms to figure out how much of each ingredient a factory needs.

**Away from the screen.** Count 10 coins into a cup, then split them into two cups. The total is still 10.

## Lesson 17: Fire is a reaction

*Challenge · about 25 minutes*

**Big idea.** Burning is a reaction between a fuel and oxygen. Burning a fuel made of carbon and hydrogen makes carbon dioxide, water and heat.

**Words**

- **combustion**: Burning: a fuel reacts with oxygen and gives off heat.

**Try it in the game**

1. Find methane, oxygen, CO₂ and water.
2. Put one CH₄ and two O₂ on the bench and press Spark.
3. Try the propane reaction, which needs five oxygen molecules.

**Check yourself**

- What does burning methane make?  
  *Answer: Carbon dioxide and water.*
- Why does a fire need air?  
  *Answer: Air has the oxygen the fuel reacts with.*

**In real life.** Gas stoves, camp stoves and car engines all use combustion. Always have an adult handle real fire.

**Away from the screen.** With an adult, light a candle and cover it with a jar. Why does it go out?

## Lesson 18: Smells and flavors

*Challenge · about 30 minutes*

**Big idea.** You smell something when its molecules reach your nose. Many flavors and smells come from molecules with rings and small groups on them, like the –OH and C=O groups in vanillin.

**Words**

- **ring**: Atoms connected in a circle.
- **benzene ring**: A flat ring of six carbons.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build benzene: six carbons in a ring with alternating double bonds.
2. Build vanillin by adding –OH, –OCH₃ and –CHO to the ring.
3. Rotate vanillin and find the ring and each group.

**Check yourself**

- Which smell is vanillin?  
  *Answer: Vanilla.*
- What shape is a benzene ring?  
  *Answer: A flat hexagon.*

**In real life.** Vanilla flavor, many perfumes and many dyes come from molecules built on rings.

**Away from the screen.** Smell vanilla extract, lemon and mint. Each smell is a different molecule.

## Lesson 19: Chemistry inside you

*Challenge · about 30 minutes*

**Big idea.** Your body runs on molecules. Glucose is a sugar your cells use for energy. Amino acids like glycine link together to make proteins. Vitamin C helps your body build collagen in skin.

**Words**

- **protein**: A long chain of amino acids folded into a shape.
- **amino acid**: A building block of proteins.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build glycine, glucose and vitamin C.
2. In the Atlas, compare their shapes in the Ball & Stick view.
3. Find the –OH groups on glucose and vitamin C.

**Check yourself**

- What does your body use glucose for?  
  *Answer: Energy.*
- What do amino acids make?  
  *Answer: Proteins.*

**In real life.** Eating fruit, bread and eggs gives you glucose, vitamin C and amino acids.

**Away from the screen.** Make a food diary page: write which food has sugar, protein or vitamin C.

## Lesson 20: Patterns in the elements

*Challenge · about 30 minutes*

**Big idea.** Elements in the same column of the periodic table behave alike. Oxygen and sulfur both make 2 bonds, so H₂O and H₂S have the same bent shape. Fluorine and chlorine both make 1 bond, so NaF and NaCl are alike.

**Words**

- **periodic table**: A chart that arranges the elements so that similar ones line up.
- **family (group)**: A column of elements with similar behavior.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build hydrogen sulfide, H₂S, and compare its shape with water.
2. Build sodium fluoride and compare it with table salt.
3. Predict: what would the molecule made from hydrogen and fluorine look like? Why?

**Check yourself**

- Why are H₂O and H₂S shaped alike?  
  *Answer: Oxygen and sulfur are in the same family and each makes 2 bonds with 2 lone pairs.*
- Which two elements in the game are like chlorine?  
  *Answer: Fluorine is in the same family.*

**In real life.** Chemists predict how new materials will behave by using patterns like these.

**Away from the screen.** Sort a pile of buttons by color, size and holes. Different ways of sorting show different patterns, just like the periodic table.
