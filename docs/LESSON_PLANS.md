# Atlas' Atoms: 31 Lesson Plans

Written for a very advanced 7-year-old working with a grown-up. A path from "what is an atom?" to burning fuel, acids and the chemistry of your body. Each lesson takes 10 to 20 minutes and is built around the game. The same lessons appear in the game under **Lessons**, and the game ticks a lesson off automatically when the child finishes its activity.

**For the adult:** let the child do the building. Ask "what do you think will happen?" before each step. The off-screen activities use ordinary household items. Do them with an adult nearby, and never taste or sniff unknown chemicals.

## Lesson 1: Everything is made of atoms

*Start here · about 10 minutes*

**Big idea.** Everything is made of atoms. Atoms are so tiny that you cannot see them. One hair on your head is hundreds of thousands of atoms wide.

**Words**

- **atom**: A tiny piece of stuff. Everything is built from atoms.
- **matter**: Anything you can touch or hold.

**Try it in the game**

1. Drag one white H atom onto the bench.
2. Spin the view with your mouse. Scroll to zoom.
3. Click the H button on the left (do not drag) to read about hydrogen.

**Check yourself**

- Can you see atoms with your eyes?  
  *Answer: No. They are too tiny.*
- Name something that is made of atoms.  
  *Answer: Anything works: a rock, water, air, you.*

**In real life.** The air, the water you drink and your own body are all made of atoms.

**Away from the screen.** Walk around and find 10 things. Each one is made of atoms.

**Grown-up question.** What do you think is inside a rock? Inside a puddle? Inside you?

## Lesson 2: Meet the elements

*Start here · about 10 minutes*

**Big idea.** An element is one kind of atom. Each element has a short code called a symbol. H is hydrogen. C is carbon. N is nitrogen. O is oxygen.

**Words**

- **element**: One kind of atom, like all hydrogen.
- **symbol**: A short code for an element, like H or O.

**Try it in the game**

1. Click H, C, N and O on the left to open their cards.
2. Look at each atom's color. Chemists use these colors too.
3. Find the number called atomic number on each card.

**Check yourself**

- What is the symbol for oxygen?  
  *Answer: O.*
- What is the symbol for carbon?  
  *Answer: C.*

**In real life.** Every chemist in every country uses the same symbols.

**Away from the screen.** Make four cards: H, C, N, O. Write the name on the back.

**Grown-up question.** Quiz game: say a symbol and let them say the element. Then swap.

## Lesson 3: Atoms have hands

*Start here · about 10 minutes*

**Big idea.** Each atom likes to hold a certain number of other atoms. We call these its hands. Hydrogen has 1 hand. Oxygen has 2. Nitrogen has 3. Carbon has 4. Chemists call this number the valence.

**Words**

- **bond**: A link that holds two atoms together.
- **valence**: How many bonds an atom likes to make.

**Try it in the game**

1. Drag two H atoms onto the bench.
2. Drop one on top of the other to make a bond.
3. Watch the dots under each atom. Green means a hand is used. When all hands are used, the atom smiles.

**Check yourself**

- How many hands does oxygen have?  
  *Answer: Two.*
- Why did the hydrogens smile?  
  *Answer: Each has one hand, and the bond used it.*

**In real life.** Two hydrogens joined together make hydrogen gas. It can fill balloons and fuel rockets.

**Away from the screen.** Hold hands with someone. You each used one hand, just like two hydrogens.

**Grown-up question.** If an atom has 3 hands, how many other atoms can it hold?

## Lesson 4: Sharing makes a bond

*Start here · about 10 minutes*

**Big idea.** Atoms hold on to each other by sharing tiny bits called electrons. One shared pair is a single bond. Two shared pairs make a double bond. The oxygen you breathe has a double bond.

**Words**

- **electron**: A tiny bit that atoms share to make a bond.
- **double bond**: Two shared pairs between the same two atoms.

**Try it in the game**

1. Drag two O atoms. Drop one on the other to make a bond.
2. Tap the gold + on the bond (or drop one O on the other again) to make a double bond. Now both smile.
3. Click Electrons in the view buttons. Count the lines between the O atoms.

**Check yourself**

- What do atoms share to make a bond?  
  *Answer: Electrons.*
- How many lines are between the O atoms?  
  *Answer: Two. That is a double bond.*

**In real life.** Every breath of oxygen you take is made of atoms joined by a double bond.

**Away from the screen.** Link two paper clips once. Then link them twice. Which is harder to pull apart?

**Grown-up question.** Say: Sharing is how atoms stay together. Ask what else we hold on to by sharing.

## Lesson 5: Your first molecule: water

*Start here · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** A molecule is atoms joined together. A formula tells you which atoms are in it. H₂O means 2 hydrogens and 1 oxygen. The little number tells you how many.

**Words**

- **molecule**: Two or more atoms joined together.
- **formula**: A short way to write which atoms are in a molecule.

**Try it in the game**

1. Drag one O atom onto the bench.
2. Drop two H atoms on it, one at a time.
3. Look at the formula under 'On your bench'. It says H₂O.
4. Click an atom to poke it and read about it.

**Check yourself**

- What does the little 2 in H₂O mean?  
  *Answer: There are 2 hydrogen atoms.*
- How many atoms are in one water molecule?  
  *Answer: Three.*

**In real life.** Oceans, rain and your body are mostly water.

**Away from the screen.** Fill a glass with water. It holds more water molecules than there are stars you can see.

**Grown-up question.** What is the formula for two water molecules? (2 H₂O means 4 hydrogens and 2 oxygens.)

## Lesson 6: Shape matters

*Getting good · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** Molecules have shapes. Water is bent, like a boomerang. Carbon dioxide is straight, like a stick. The shape depends on how the atoms push on each other.

**Words**

- **lone pair**: Two electrons on an atom that are not in a bond.
- **straight**: Atoms in a line. Chemists say linear.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build water and turn it around to see the bend.
2. Click Orbitals. The gold lobes on the O are lone pairs. They push the hydrogens close.
3. Build CO₂: one C with a double bond to two O atoms. Is it bent or straight?

**Check yourself**

- Is water bent or straight?  
  *Answer: Bent.*
- Is carbon dioxide bent or straight?  
  *Answer: Straight.*

**In real life.** Water's bent shape is a big reason ice floats and salt can dissolve in it.

**Away from the screen.** Use marshmallows and toothpicks to make a bent molecule and a straight one.

**Grown-up question.** Why do you think the gold lobes make the hydrogens squeeze together?

## Lesson 7: The air around you

*Getting good · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** Air is mostly two molecules. About 78 out of every 100 are nitrogen, N₂. About 21 out of every 100 are oxygen, O₂. A tiny bit is carbon dioxide, CO₂.

**Words**

- **gas**: Molecules flying around with lots of room.
- **triple bond**: Three shared pairs between the same two atoms.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build N₂: drop one N on another three times to make a triple bond.
2. Compare the O₂ and N₂ bonds in the Electrons view.
3. Open My Atlas and read about N₂, O₂ and CO₂.

**Check yourself**

- What is most of the air?  
  *Answer: Nitrogen.*
- How many shared pairs are in N₂?  
  *Answer: Three, a triple bond.*

**In real life.** You breathe in oxygen and breathe out carbon dioxide. Most of each breath is nitrogen.

**Away from the screen.** Wave a piece of paper and feel the air push back.

**Grown-up question.** Why can't we see air if it is made of molecules?

## Lesson 8: Carbon has four hands

*Getting good · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** Carbon has four hands, so it can hold lots of atoms. That makes carbon the main building block of living things.

**Words**

- **tetrahedron**: A shape like a tiny pyramid with four points.
- **methane**: One carbon with four hydrogens.

**Try it in the game**

1. Drag one C onto the bench.
2. Drop four H atoms on it.
3. Turn it around. The H atoms point to the corners of a tiny pyramid.

**Check yourself**

- How many hands does carbon have?  
  *Answer: Four.*
- What is the formula for methane?  
  *Answer: CH₄.*

**In real life.** Methane is the main part of the natural gas that heats many homes.

**Away from the screen.** Make a pyramid from 4 gumdrops and 6 toothpicks.

**Grown-up question.** Why do you think the four H atoms spread out instead of bunching up?

## Lesson 9: Chains of carbon

*Getting good · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** Carbon atoms can hold on to each other and make a chain. Fill the rest of the hands with hydrogen. Propane has three carbons in a row.

**Words**

- **chain**: Atoms joined one after another.
- **fuel**: Something we burn to get heat.

**Try it in the game**

1. Join three C atoms in a row.
2. Fill every open hand with an H. You need 8 H atoms.
3. Turn it. Why does the chain zig-zag?

**Check yourself**

- How many H atoms are in propane?  
  *Answer: Eight.*
- Why does the chain zig-zag?  
  *Answer: Each carbon's four bonds point to the corners of a tiny pyramid.*

**In real life.** Propane is the fuel in camp stoves and grills.

**Away from the screen.** Link paper clips into a chain. Try to make it perfectly straight.

**Grown-up question.** How many H atoms would a chain of four carbons need? (10, and that is butane.)

## Lesson 10: Double and triple bonds

*Getting good · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** Carbons can share more than one pair. A double bond is shorter and stronger than a single bond. A triple bond is even shorter and stronger. A double bond keeps atoms flat. A triple bond makes a straight line.

**Words**

- **flat**: All the atoms sit on one flat sheet.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build ethylene: two C atoms with a double bond and two H on each.
2. Build acetylene: two C atoms with a triple bond and one H on each.
3. Which one is flat? Which one is a line?

**Check yourself**

- Which bond is shorter, single or double?  
  *Answer: Double.*
- How many H atoms are in acetylene?  
  *Answer: Two.*

**In real life.** Ripe bananas give off ethylene, and it makes nearby fruit ripen faster.

**Away from the screen.** Put a ripe banana in a paper bag with a green tomato. Check each day.

**Grown-up question.** Why do you think carbon needs fewer H atoms when it has more bonds to another carbon?

## Lesson 11: Same atoms, different molecule

*Big kid chemistry · about 20 minutes*

**Big idea.** The same atoms can join in different ways to make different molecules. These are called isomers. Ethanol and dimethyl ether both have 2 C, 6 H and 1 O. Ethanol is a liquid. Dimethyl ether is a gas.

**Words**

- **isomer**: A molecule that has the same atoms as another but joined in a different way.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build ethanol: C–C–O, with H atoms on every other hand.
2. Now build the same atoms as C–O–C. The bench says: right atoms, different arrangement.
3. Can you find a third way? (Hint: you cannot. Try!)

**Check yourself**

- Can two molecules have the same atoms but be different?  
  *Answer: Yes. How they are joined matters.*
- What is an isomer?  
  *Answer: Same atoms, different arrangement.*

**In real life.** Medicines can work very differently when the atoms are joined in a different way.

**Away from the screen.** Build two different things from the same 6 LEGO bricks.

**Grown-up question.** What is the same about two isomers? What is different?

## Lesson 12: Salt: giving, not sharing

*Big kid chemistry · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** In water, atoms share. In salt, sodium gives away one electron to chlorine. Now sodium is positive and chlorine is negative. Opposite charges stick together. This is an ionic bond. The game draws it as a dashed line.

**Words**

- **ion**: An atom with a charge because it gave or took an electron.
- **ionic bond**: Positive and negative atoms sticking together.

**Try it in the game**

1. Find enough molecules to unlock Na and Cl.
2. Drop Na onto Cl.
3. Look for the dashed line and the + and − signs.

**Check yourself**

- What does sodium do with its electron?  
  *Answer: It gives it to chlorine.*
- What is the dashed line?  
  *Answer: An ionic bond.*

**In real life.** Table salt is sodium chloride. A grain of salt is a crystal of billions of ions.

**Away from the screen.** Stir a spoon of salt into warm water until it disappears. Do not drink it.

**Grown-up question.** How is giving different from sharing?

## Lesson 13: Sour acids

*Big kid chemistry · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** Acids taste sour. Vinegar has acetic acid. Lemons have citric acid. Fizzy water has a little carbonic acid. Many acids have a COOH group on them.

**Words**

- **acid**: Something that tastes sour and can give away a hydrogen.
- **group**: A small bunch of atoms stuck on a molecule, like COOH.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build acetic acid. Find the COOH end in the Electrons view.
2. Build carbonic acid.
3. Compare them.

**Check yourself**

- What makes vinegar sour?  
  *Answer: Acetic acid.*
- Where do you find citric acid?  
  *Answer: Lemons and limes.*

**In real life.** Acids give lemons, vinegar and many candies their tang. Never taste unknown chemicals.

**Away from the screen.** With a grown-up, put a spoon of baking soda in a cup and add a little vinegar. The bubbles are carbon dioxide.

**Grown-up question.** What is bubbling when baking soda meets vinegar? (Carbon dioxide gas.)

## Lesson 14: Heat makes atoms move

*Big kid chemistry · about 10 minutes*

**Big idea.** Heat makes molecules move faster. In ice they just wiggle in place. In water they slide around. In steam they zoom everywhere.

**Words**

- **temperature**: How fast the tiny pieces are moving.
- **solid, liquid, gas**: Three ways stuff can be: hard, runny or airy.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build a molecule or two on the bench.
2. Slide the Heat slider all the way up.
3. Press Shake. Then turn Heat down.

**Check yourself**

- What does heat do to molecules?  
  *Answer: Makes them move faster.*
- Which has molecules moving most, ice, water or steam?  
  *Answer: Steam.*

**In real life.** Ice melts because heat makes water molecules move enough to slide.

**Away from the screen.** Put an ice cube in warm water and another in cold water. Which melts faster?

**Grown-up question.** Why does a hot drink stop steaming as it cools?

## Lesson 15: Reactions rearrange atoms

*Big kid chemistry · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** In a reaction, bonds break and new bonds form. The atoms are the same, but they join up in new ways to make new molecules.

**Words**

- **reaction**: Atoms joining up in new ways.
- **product**: The new molecule you make.

**Try it in the game**

1. Find hydrogen, oxygen and water.
2. Open My Atlas and put two H₂ and one O₂ on the bench.
3. Press Spark in the Reactions box and watch.

**Check yourself**

- What do you start with in 2 H₂ + O₂?  
  *Answer: Hydrogen and oxygen.*
- What do you make?  
  *Answer: Water.*

**In real life.** Rockets can burn hydrogen with oxygen. All you get out is water.

**Away from the screen.** Take apart a LEGO model and build a new one with the same bricks.

**Grown-up question.** Where did the water's atoms come from?

## Lesson 16: Atoms never disappear

*Challenge · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** In a reaction, no atoms are made and no atoms vanish. You end up with exactly the same atoms you started with, just joined differently. This is called conservation of mass.

**Words**

- **conservation of mass**: The atoms never disappear or appear.

**Try it in the game**

1. Do two different reactions from the Reactions box.
2. Before each Spark, count the atoms on the bench.
3. After Spark, count again. Same numbers!

**Check yourself**

- Do atoms disappear in a reaction?  
  *Answer: No.*
- How many O atoms are in 2 H₂O?  
  *Answer: Two.*

**In real life.** Chemists count atoms to know how much of each ingredient to use.

**Away from the screen.** Put 10 coins in a cup. Split them into two cups. There are still 10.

**Grown-up question.** If you start with 4 H atoms, how many H atoms must you end with?

## Lesson 17: Fire is a reaction

*Challenge · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** Burning is a reaction between a fuel and oxygen. It makes heat. When a fuel made of carbon and hydrogen burns, it makes carbon dioxide and water.

**Words**

- **burning**: A fuel joining with oxygen and giving off heat.

**Try it in the game**

1. Find methane, oxygen, carbon dioxide and water.
2. Put one CH₄ and two O₂ on the bench. Press Spark.
3. Try propane: it needs five O₂.

**Check yourself**

- What does burning methane make?  
  *Answer: Carbon dioxide and water.*
- Why does fire need air?  
  *Answer: Air has the oxygen the fuel needs.*

**In real life.** Gas stoves, camp stoves and car engines all burn fuel. Only grown-ups handle real fire.

**Away from the screen.** With a grown-up, light a candle and cover it with a jar. Watch what happens.

**Grown-up question.** Why does the candle go out under the jar?

## Lesson 20: Patterns in the elements

*Challenge · about 20 minutes*

**Big idea.** Elements in the same column of the periodic table act alike. Oxygen and sulfur both have 2 hands, so water, H₂O, and H₂S are both bent. Fluorine and chlorine both have 1 hand, so NaF and NaCl are alike.

**Words**

- **periodic table**: A chart that puts similar elements together.
- **family**: A column of elements that act alike.

**Try it in the game**

1. Build H₂S and compare its shape with water.
2. Build NaF and compare it with salt.
3. Guess: what would H and F make?

**Check yourself**

- Why are H₂O and H₂S shaped alike?  
  *Answer: Oxygen and sulfur are in the same family and both have 2 hands.*
- Which element is chlorine's family member in the game?  
  *Answer: Fluorine.*

**In real life.** Chemists use patterns like these to guess how new materials will behave.

**Away from the screen.** Sort buttons by color, then by size, then by holes. Different sorting shows different patterns.

**Grown-up question.** What is another pattern you can find in the tray of atoms?

## Lesson 21: Electrons live in clouds

*Deep dive · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** An electron is not a tiny ball zooming around like a planet. It is better to picture a fuzzy cloud. The cloud is thick where the electron is likely to be. Chemists call each cloud shape an orbital. The simplest one, s, is a ball.

**Words**

- **orbital**: A cloud shape that shows where an electron is likely to be.
- **node**: A spot inside an orbital where the electron is never found.

**Try it in the game**

1. Open the Molecule Lab (the microscope button) and choose the Orbitals tab.
2. Look at the 1s orbital. Each dot is one place the electron might be.
3. Press Dots, then Shape, then both. Spin it around.
4. Now press 2s. Find the ball inside a bigger ball. The gap between them is a node.

**Check yourself**

- What is an orbital?  
  *Answer: A cloud shape where an electron is likely to be.*
- What shape is an s orbital?  
  *Answer: A ball.*

**In real life.** Hydrogen, the atom in every water molecule, has one electron in a 1s cloud.

**Away from the screen.** Spin a fan or a pencil very fast. It looks like a blurry shape instead of a single thing. An electron cloud is a little like that.

**Grown-up question.** Why do you think chemists draw clouds instead of tiny circles?

## Lesson 22: Dumbbells: the p orbitals

*Deep dive · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** Bigger atoms also have p orbitals. A p orbital looks like a dumbbell with two lobes. There are three of them, pointing along x, y and z at right angles. The blue and orange colors show phase, like the up and down of a wave.

**Words**

- **lobe**: One round part of an orbital.
- **phase**: Whether the wave is up (blue) or down (orange).

**Try it in the game**

1. In the Lab, Orbitals tab, press 2p (one).
2. Find the two lobes and the node in the middle.
3. Press 2p (all three) and spin it. Which way does each one point?

**Check yourself**

- What shape is a p orbital?  
  *Answer: A dumbbell with two lobes.*
- How many p orbitals are in a set?  
  *Answer: Three.*

**In real life.** Carbon, nitrogen and oxygen use p orbitals to make their bonds.

**Away from the screen.** Hold three pencils at right angles, like the corner of a box. Those are x, y and z.

**Grown-up question.** What is the same about the three p orbitals? What is different?

## Lesson 23: Mixing orbitals: sp³

*Deep dive · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** A carbon atom mixes its s orbital and its three p orbitals into four new matching orbitals called sp³. They point to the corners of a tiny pyramid. This is why methane and so many carbon molecules have that pyramid shape.

**Words**

- **hybrid**: A new orbital made by mixing others.
- **sp³**: One s and three p orbitals mixed together.

**Try it in the game**

1. In the Lab, Orbitals tab, press sp³ hybrid.
2. Press Mix! and watch the ball and dumbbells turn into four lobes.
3. Spin it. The white balls are hydrogens. Where do the lobes point?

**Check yourself**

- What does carbon mix to make sp³ orbitals?  
  *Answer: One s and three p orbitals.*
- Where do the four sp³ orbitals point?  
  *Answer: To the corners of a tiny pyramid.*

**In real life.** Every carbon with four single bonds, in methane, propane and sugar, uses sp³ orbitals.

**Away from the screen.** Mix blue and yellow paint to get green. Mixing makes something new, like mixing orbitals does.

**Grown-up question.** Why might four matching orbitals be better for making four bonds?

## Lesson 24: The π bond: why double bonds stay flat

*Deep dive · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** In ethene, the sticks are σ bonds, made by orbitals meeting head to head. The leftover p orbitals meet side by side above and below. That cloud is a π bond. To twist the molecule you would have to pull the π cloud apart, so the double bond stays flat.

**Words**

- **σ bond (sigma)**: A bond where orbitals meet head to head.
- **π bond (pi)**: A bond where p orbitals meet side by side.

**Try it in the game**

1. In the Lab, Orbitals tab, press π bond.
2. Find the sticks (σ bonds) and the blue and orange clouds (π bond).
3. Spin it. Is the molecule flat?

**Check yourself**

- Where is the π bond cloud?  
  *Answer: Above and below the atoms.*
- Why is the C=C double bond in ethene flat?  
  *Answer: Twisting would pull the π cloud apart.*

**In real life.** The flat double bond in the molecule that gives ripe bananas their signal, ethene, comes from its π bond.

**Away from the screen.** Hold two pencils side by side with a rubber band around them. You cannot twist one without the band resisting.

**Grown-up question.** What do you think happens to a molecule if the π bond breaks?

## Lesson 25: Molecular orbitals: two become two

*Deep dive · about 20 minutes*

**Big idea.** When two atoms come close, their orbitals overlap and make two molecular orbitals. Adding the waves makes the bonding orbital, with electrons piled up between the nuclei. Subtracting them makes the antibonding orbital, with a node in the middle. Bond order = (bonding electrons − antibonding electrons) ÷ 2.

**Words**

- **bonding orbital**: A molecular orbital that holds atoms together.
- **antibonding orbital**: A molecular orbital that pushes atoms apart.
- **bond order**: How many bonds: (bonding − antibonding) ÷ 2.

**Try it in the game**

1. In the Lab, open Molecular orbitals.
2. Look at the σ bonding orbital. Where are most dots?
3. Press σ* antibonding. Find the empty plane in the middle.
4. Slide the distance. When do the orbitals overlap the most?

**Check yourself**

- What do you get when two atomic orbitals overlap?  
  *Answer: Two molecular orbitals.*
- Where are the electrons in a bonding orbital?  
  *Answer: Between the two nuclei.*

**In real life.** Chemists use molecular orbitals to design new materials, medicines and solar cells.

**Away from the screen.** Drop two pebbles in a pond near each other. The ripples add up in some places and cancel in others, a bit like orbitals do.

**Grown-up question.** What do you think happens to the overlap when the atoms are very far apart?

## Lesson 26: Why helium stays alone

*Deep dive · about 15 minutes*

**Big idea.** Use the electron counter to fill the orbitals. Two electrons go in the bonding orbital: a strong bond, bond order 1. With four electrons, both orbitals are full. The bonding and antibonding electrons cancel, so the bond order is zero. That is why helium atoms do not stick together.

**Words**

- **noble gas**: An element like helium that does not like to bond.

**Try it in the game**

1. In the Lab, Molecular orbitals tab, slide Electrons to 1, 2, 3 and 4.
2. Watch the arrows fill the diagram and the bond order change.
3. Which count gives bond order 0? What does that mean?

**Check yourself**

- What is the bond order of H₂?  
  *Answer: One.*
- Why doesn't helium make He₂?  
  *Answer: The bonding and antibonding electrons cancel.*

**In real life.** Helium floats in party balloons as single atoms, because it never joins up with itself.

**Away from the screen.** Tug-of-war with two equal teams goes nowhere. Bonding and antibonding electrons are like two equal teams.

**Grown-up question.** Why do you think hydrogen forms H₂ but helium does not form He₂?

## Lesson 27: Reactions start with a crash

*Advanced · about 20 minutes*

**Big idea.** For molecules to react, they have to bump into each other. A gentle bump does nothing. The bump has to be hard enough, and the molecules have to hit the right way around. Only then do old bonds break and new bonds form.

**Words**

- **collision**: Two molecules bumping into each other.
- **collision theory**: The idea that a reaction needs a hard enough bump, the right way around.

**Try it in the game**

1. Open the Molecule Lab and choose How reactions happen.
2. Watch the red pairs and green pairs zoom around the box.
3. Look at the numbers under the box. How many bumps were too gentle? How many were the wrong way round?
4. Wait for a reaction. A red pair and a green pair swap partners and flash gold.

**Check yourself**

- What has to happen for molecules to react?  
  *Answer: They bump hard enough, the right way around.*
- What happens in a bump that is too gentle?  
  *Answer: The molecules bounce off and stay the same.*

**In real life.** Wood does not burn just because it sits in air full of oxygen. The molecules need hard enough bumps, which is why you have to light it. Real reactions can have extra steps, and this lab is a simplified model.

**Away from the screen.** Roll two toy cars into each other slowly, then fast. A slow bump just bounces. Only a hard bump does something.

**Grown-up question.** Why do you think most bumps do not make a reaction?

## Lesson 28: Breaking and making bonds

*Advanced · about 20 minutes*

**Big idea.** In a reaction, old bonds break and new bonds form. Breaking bonds takes energy in. Making bonds gives energy out. If more energy comes out than went in, the reaction gives off heat. That is exothermic. If more goes in than comes out, it takes in heat. That is endothermic.

**Words**

- **exothermic**: A reaction that gives off heat.
- **endothermic**: A reaction that takes in heat.

**Try it in the game**

1. In the Lab, How reactions happen, find the energy hill picture.
2. Slide Energy change toward 'gives off heat'. The products end lower than the reactants.
3. Slide it the other way. Now the products end higher.
4. Watch the box. When a reaction gives off heat, the new molecules zoom away faster.

**Check yourself**

- What does exothermic mean?  
  *Answer: The reaction gives off heat.*
- What happens when bonds break?  
  *Answer: Energy goes in.*

**In real life.** A campfire gives off heat and light. Plants take in light energy to make sugar, so that reaction takes in energy.

**Away from the screen.** Feel a warm hand warmer or a cold pack with a grown-up. One gives out heat, and one takes it in.

**Grown-up question.** For a grown-up: in H₂ + Cl₂ → 2 HCl, breaking H–H (about 436 kJ/mol) and Cl–Cl (about 243) costs about 679. Making two H–Cl bonds (about 431 each) returns about 862. The net is about 183 kJ/mol released. Ask: which side was bigger, breaking or making?

## Lesson 29: The energy hill

*Advanced · about 20 minutes*

**Big idea.** Even reactions that give off lots of heat need a push to start. The push is called activation energy, and we draw it as a hill. Molecules need enough energy to get over the top. A spark gives hydrogen and oxygen enough energy to get over the hill, and then they react all at once.

**Words**

- **activation energy**: The energy push needed to start a reaction.

**Try it in the game**

1. In the Lab, How reactions happen, slide Hill height up and down.
2. Set the hill high. How many bumps are hard enough now?
3. Set it low. What happens in the box?
4. Find a hill height where the reaction is slow, and one where it is fast.

**Check yourself**

- What is activation energy?  
  *Answer: The push needed to start a reaction.*
- What does a higher hill do to a reaction?  
  *Answer: Fewer bumps are hard enough, so it is slower.*

**In real life.** Hydrogen and oxygen can sit together in a tank without reacting. Add a spark and they react at once.

**Away from the screen.** Roll a ball toward a small hill and a big hill. It needs a bigger push for the big one.

**Grown-up question.** Why is a spark needed even though the reaction gives off lots of heat?

## Lesson 30: Making reactions faster

*Advanced · about 20 minutes*

**Big idea.** Three things speed up reactions. Heat makes molecules move faster, so more bumps are hard enough. Crowding puts more molecules in the same space, so there are more bumps. A catalyst is a helper that makes the hill lower. A catalyst is not used up.

**Words**

- **catalyst**: A helper that speeds up a reaction and is not used up.
- **concentration**: How crowded the molecules are.

**Try it in the game**

1. In the Lab, How reactions happen, press Start again and turn Heat up. Watch the reaction speed.
2. Turn Heat down, then tick Add a catalyst. Does it still react?
3. Try more Crowding.
4. Which one helped the most?

**Check yourself**

- What does a catalyst do?  
  *Answer: It lowers the hill so reactions are faster.*
- Why does heat speed up a reaction?  
  *Answer: Molecules move faster, so more bumps are hard enough.*

**In real life.** Enzymes in your body are catalysts that help break down food. A car has a catalytic converter that helps clean its exhaust. A fridge slows down the reactions that spoil food.

**Away from the screen.** With a grown-up, drop the same amount of sugar into warm and cold water. Which dissolves faster? Heat speeds up many changes.

**Grown-up question.** Why does food spoil more slowly in the fridge?

## Lesson 31: Balancing equations

*Advanced · about 20 minutes*

**Big idea.** A chemical equation is a recipe. The molecules you start with are on the left and the molecules you make are on the right. Atoms never disappear, so each kind of atom must have the same count on both sides. Balancing means adding numbers in front of molecules until the counts match, like 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O.

**Words**

- **equation**: A recipe for a reaction, written with formulas.
- **coefficient**: The big number in front of a molecule.

**Try it in the game**

1. Open the Reactions box on the bench and read each equation.
2. Pick CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O. Count the C, H and O on each side.
3. Put the molecules on the bench from My Atlas and press Spark.
4. Run at least four different reactions.

**Check yourself**

- How many hydrogen atoms are in 2 H₂O?  
  *Answer: Four.*
- Why must an equation balance?  
  *Answer: Atoms never disappear or appear.*

**In real life.** Chemists balance equations to know exactly how much of each ingredient a factory needs.

**Away from the screen.** Count the wheels on 3 bikes and 2 cars. Balancing is counting parts on both sides.

**Grown-up question.** If you start with 6 oxygen atoms, how many must you end with?
