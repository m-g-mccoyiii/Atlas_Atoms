# Brief for anyone evaluating Overreact

Read this before judging anything. It says what the game is trying to be, what it is measured against, and the owner's rules.

## What it is

Overreact is a roguelike score-attack game about organic chemistry, for commercial release on Steam. A run is a series of rounds. In a round the player works on one molecule over three pages of three reactions each, picking reagents that change it. Reactions that follow on from one another build a row; a page that ends on a row of three goes off by itself (the "overreact" chain). The round is won by passing a number to beat. Between rounds the player takes one of three items: a reagent, a piece of equipment, or an upgrade.

The player does not need to know chemistry. Chemistry is the fiction and the source of rules, the way poker is for Balatro.

## What it is measured against

These are the owner's references. Judge the game against them, by name.

- **Balatro.** The main reference. A simple visible formula (chips times mult) that the player can read before committing. A hand to hold, play or discard from, so every turn is a decision. Jokers that change what things are worth and combine into absurd totals. Blinds on a known curve. Scoring that counts up piece by piece so the viewer follows why the number grew. Dense, framed, tactile presentation.
- **Raccoin.** Pace and cascade. Things are always moving, one event sets off the next, the player is rarely waiting, and a streamer's audience can follow it without explanation.
- **Vampire Survivors.** Escalation and build identity. A run starts small and ends absurd. Choices between rounds compound, and by the end the screen shows what the build became.
- Also studied: Slay the Spire (characters and decks that change how a run plays), Luck be a Landlord (items that trigger each other).

The owner's phrases for the target: "addictive in a good way", "dopamine hits", "fun insane game", "entertaining to watch on a stream", "it needs to blitz". Not wanted: anything textbook-like, anything that looks like a cheap mobile ad, and anything the player has to have explained.

## What good looks like here

1. A viewer who has never seen the game understands within five seconds what the number is, what the goal is, and what the player just did.
2. Every turn is a real decision: more than one sensible option, with visible consequences, and a reason to hold back or commit.
3. A run escalates. Late rounds look and score nothing like early ones, because of what the player built.
4. Cause and effect are visible. When the score jumps, the page shows what caused it.
5. It is fast. The player is never waiting on an animation.

## The owner's rules

- Writing, in the game and in reports: plain flat sentences. No quips, no hinting, no punchlines, no em dashes, multiplication signs or middle dots. No second-person interface labels. American spelling. Almost no sentences in the game itself: objects, icons and short tokens.
- Art: must not look machine-made. Objects are 3D. Readings are 2D drawn on the objects or the page, never on a panel of their own. The ball-and-stick molecule is not restyled and atoms never get faces. Rejected looks: dark navy, uppercase letter-spaced mono labels, serif headings, glow accents, flat lab signage, cards that resemble Balatro's, hard rectangles.
- No login streaks, timers or paid currency.
- Things the owner has rejected outright: circles drawn on the molecule, particle balls, ticks and checkmarks, pulsing buttons, bursts and banners added for excitement, long explanatory labels, a "set it off" button, a burning fuse.

## How to evaluate

- Be blunt and specific. Name the element, where it is, and what is wrong.
- Judge whole frames, not crops. List every overlap, clipped number and object you cannot identify.
- Test art blind: say what you think each object is before reading its name.
- For rules and design, compare with a named mechanic in a reference game, and propose a mechanic, not a principle.
- Say what you would do first, and what you would cut.
- Do not edit files.
